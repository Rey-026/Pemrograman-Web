Popular Districts & Food

Tokyo is really 30 cities in one. Each neighbourhood has its own personality, cuisine, and vibe. Here are the ones you absolutely cannot miss.

Popular Districts

Shibuya

Home to the world's most famous pedestrian crossing, Shibuya is the beating heart of youth culture in Tokyo. When the lights turn red, up to 3,000 people cross simultaneously from every direction an organised chaos that has become one of the most photographed moments in the world. Beyond the crossing, Shibuya is packed with department stores like Hikarie and Scramble Square, independent boutiques in Cat Street, and some of the city's best rooftop bars.

Best time to visit: Dusk on a weekday the neon hits differently, and the crowds are slightly thinner than weekends.

Asakusa

Step into Asakusa and you step back in time. This is the oldest part of Tokyo a neighbourhood that somehow survived both the 1923 earthquake and the wartime bombings. The iconic Senso-ji Temple, Tokyo's oldest Buddhist temple, anchors the district, approached through the thundering Kaminarimon Gate with its massive red lantern. The Nakamise shopping street leading up to it is lined with stalls selling ningyo-yaki cakes, samurai swords, and folding fans.

Insider tip: Arrive before 8 am to experience the temple in near-silence with the morning light streaming through the incense smoke.

Akihabara

Known as "Electric Town," Akihabara is the global epicentre of anime, manga, retro gaming, and consumer electronics. Multi-storey buildings are stacked floor-to-floor with everything from the latest GPU to vintage Famicom cartridges. The district has also evolved into a hub for maid cafés, crane game arcades, and limited-edition merchandise that you simply cannot find anywhere else on Earth.

Tips for Visiting Akihabara

Best visited on a Sunday when Chuo-dori closes to cars between noon and 6 pm, turning the main boulevard into a giant pedestrian zone. Bring more cash than you think you'll need you will absolutely find something to buy.

Shinjuku

Shinjuku Station is the busiest railway station in the world, handling over 3.5 million passengers daily. But the real magic is outside the station. To the east, Kabukicho blazes with neon Tokyo's famous entertainment district. To the west, gleaming skyscrapers house the Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building (free observation deck!). And tucked between them, Golden Gai a tiny grid of alleys lined with barely 50-seat bars, each with its own theme and regulars.

Harajuku

Harajuku is the birthplace of Japanese street fashion a district that has been exporting its wildly creative style to the rest of the world for decades. Takeshita Street is a sensory overload of crepe shops, thrift stores, and teenagers in outfits that defy categorisation. A five-minute walk away, the serene Meiji Shrine sits inside 70 hectares of forested parkland a perfect reminder of how Tokyo holds opposites so gracefully.

Famous Tokyo Food

Tokyo has more Michelin starred restaurants than any other city on earth but some of the best meals you'll eat here cost less than a coffee back home. Here's what to look for.

  • Ramen — Tokyo style soy-based broth with thin noodles and a perfectly soft-boiled egg. Every ramen shop is different many have a single specialty they've perfected over decades. Expect to queue. It is always worth it.
  • Sushi — From high-end omakase (chef's choice) counters where a single meal costs ¥30,000, to conveyor belt kaiten-zushi where a plate of two pieces costs ¥100. Both are excellent. The fish at Tsukiji Outer Market at 7 am is an experience unto itself.
  • Tempura — Lightly battered, crispy-fried seafood and vegetables served with a dipping broth. The batter is so thin it's almost translucent. Tendon (tempura over rice) is a lunchtime staple at a fraction of the price of a full tempura kaiseki course.
  • Yakitori — Skewered, charcoal-grilled chicken in every form imaginable: thigh, breast, skin, cartilage, liver. Best enjoyed at a standing bar under the train tracks in Yurakucho with a cold Sapporo draft.
  • Onigiri — The humble rice ball, available at every convenience store from ¥100. Don't sleep on 7-Eleven onigiri — they are genuinely outstanding and come in dozens of seasonal flavours.
